In practice

What the work actually changes.

Product terms that stopped costing deals

The problem

A product business whose standard terms had grown organically over years — inconsistent, poorly drafted, and no longer matching what the business actually delivered. Every deal stalled in the same place: customers wouldn’t sign without amendments, so each contract became its own negotiation.

What I did

Rewrote them into a single set of terms built to be signed first time — reflecting the services as genuinely delivered, protecting the business properly, and pitched so a reasonable customer could sign without routing it via their own lawyers.

The outcome

Amendment requests largely disappeared, deals closed faster, and the terms turned from a friction point into a competitive advantage — winning business from competitors whose paperwork still triggered a legal review on every deal.

6 weeks → under 2average time to signature
~80% → under 10%of deals needing amendments

Getting the regulator to say yes once, not every time

The problem

A regulator with a reputation for being hard to satisfy required specific outcomes in every contract between the business and its third-party suppliers. Worse, the feedback wasn’t consistent — a different reviewer would take a different view from their colleague on materially the same terms. Every new supplier deal became an unpredictable approval, impossible to plan around.

What I did

Rather than keep submitting contracts one at a time and hoping, I drafted a standardised set of regulatory provisions and took them to the regulator for approval in principle. Once agreed, they became the fixed basis for every supplier contract — and a lever in the negotiation itself: accept these as drafted and the deal clears approval quickly, so you start earning sooner.

The outcome

Suppliers had a commercial reason to agree rather than argue. Regulatory submissions fell away, approval stopped being the bottleneck on growth, and the business could onboard suppliers at the pace it actually needed to scale.

95%+of supplier deals signable first time
One pre-approvalinstead of one per contract

What an in-house hire actually costs

A first in-house lawyer at a startup or scale-up typically lands somewhere between £110,000 and £150,000 base salary, depending on seniority.

Fully loaded, that's more than the salary. On £110,000: add ~£15,750 employer NI, ~£5,500 pension and ~£11,000 of other on-costs (benefits, kit, recruitment amortised, admin) and you're at roughly £142,000 a year. On £150,000, the same build-up reaches roughly £194,000 a year.

And it doesn't flex. That cost is committed whether it's a heavy quarter or a quiet one, whether the work is specialist or routine, and whether or not there's a full month of legal work in the pipeline. A retainer scales down when you don't need it; a salary doesn't.

The figures above also leave things out. They don't include maternity or paternity leave (full pay continuing, plus the cost of covering the role), the cost of replacing someone if it doesn't work out (notice pay, recruitment, a gap in legal cover in the meantime), extended sick leave, or the time spent managing, training and developing them.

None of which means hiring in-house is wrong — at sufficient, steady volume it's often the right answer. It's a question of whether your legal workload is genuinely full-time and predictable yet.

Example catch — the cap that doesn't cap

"Each party's total liability shall not exceed the Fees paid in the preceding 12 months, for claims under this Agreement or under any indemnity given by that party."

The words in gold are what was missing. Without them, the cap doesn't touch indemnity claims.

AI drafts routinely leave indemnity clauses outside the liability cap without saying so. A cap that looks watertight can still leave you exposed to unlimited liability through an indemnity.
